About you

Your name goes at the top of the exported files and the report, so whoever reads one knows whose it is.

The appointment hours are the contract total, before anything is taken off. It is what the dashboard measures your weeks against, so a wrong number here makes every later comparison wrong.

Reserved hours and context

Reserved hours come off the appointment before regular duties, so they are not counted as time available for the course you assist. If nothing is reserved, leave it at 0. 879 In COMPSCI 879 three hours a week are encumbered for the course itself, so a 10-hour TA has 7 for regular duties and a 20-hour TA has 17.

Choosing COMPSCI 879 fills in the three reserved hours and the Fall 2026 appointment dates, and turns on the reminders about weekly uploads to Canvas. Leave it on the general setting for any other appointment and nothing about 879 appears anywhere.

Appointment dates

These set the span of the calendar on the dashboard and head the report, so a reader can see the period your hours sit inside. Days outside them are left blank on the calendar. 879 Fall 2026 runs Sunday 6 September 2026 to Saturday 16 January 2027.

Courses you are assisting

Most TAs have one. If your appointment is split across two, add both and give each the hours allocated to it. Every entry is then tagged with its course, the dashboard breaks the totals down, and you can produce a report for one instructor covering only their course.

Keeping your journal safe

Your journal is saved in this browser automatically. Browser storage can be cleared without warning, and a term-long journal is worth more than that. Linking a file writes it to a real file on your disk every time it changes; keep that file somewhere that syncs and it follows you between machines. Chrome and Edge support this; on other browsers download the JSON regularly instead.

Reminders

The panel at the top of the Log tab always shows what is outstanding, and it refreshes itself as the day moves on. Browser notifications are optional on top of that: at most one a day, only while this tab is sitting in the background, and only when something is actually due. They stop the moment you close the tab, so this is a nudge rather than a system.

How this works, and why bother

Five minutes of reading. Worth it once, at the start.

The short version

Log what you do as you do it. Once a week, look at the dashboard and write two lines about how the week went. At the end of the term you will have something almost no TA has: a record of what the job actually was, in your own words, with numbers attached.

Why a journal rather than a timesheet

A timesheet answers one question, badly: how many hours. This asks two more that turn out to matter far more. What did you actually do, and what did you notice while doing it.

That second field is the whole point. “Ran help session, 90 minutes” is a number. “Ran help session, 90 minutes, three students had the same misconception about pass-by-reference” is the start of a change to how the next one runs. One of those compounds over a semester and the other does not.

This is the difference between teaching for five years and teaching the same year five times. The people who get better at this are not the ones who work hardest. They are the ones who wrote something down while it was still fresh, looked at it later, and changed one thing.

Why it is worth it for you

You will know whether the job is what you were told it was. Most TAs have no real idea whether they are working six hours or sixteen until something goes wrong late in the term, and by then the conversation is a complaint rather than a question. A weekly number turns “this feels like a lot” into “this is the fourth week over my hours, here is where the time went.” That is a conversation an instructor can act on.

It is evidence when you need it. If anyone ever asks what you have been doing, or you need to make a case about workload, the report gives you a page of specifics rather than an impression.

It is the raw material for everything that comes later. Teaching statements, job applications, and interviews all ask for a concrete example of something you tried and what happened. Almost nobody can produce one, because almost nobody wrote anything down at the time. Your journal is forty of them.

It makes you better at the job while you are still doing it. Noticing is a skill, and it is trainable. Writing one line about what you observed, twice a week, changes what you see in the room within a month.

Using it

Set yourself up first. On the Setup tab, put in your appointment hours, anything reserved off the top, and the course or courses you are assisting. If your appointment is split across two courses, add both with the hours allocated to each; every entry then gets tagged and you can report on them separately.

Log as you go. The Log tab. Date, minutes, category, one line on what you did. Estimates are fine and a round number is fine. Do it right after a shift if you can; two minutes then beats an hour of reconstruction in December. A blank week is worse than a rough one.

Fill in what you noticed when there is something to say. Not every entry needs it. The ones that do are the ones you will reread.

Once a week, look at the dashboard. It shows where your hours went and flags in red any week that ran past the hours available for regular duties. Then the This week tab: did it fit, and is anything worth flagging. Two lines.

Fill in What I know early. Ten things every TA should be able to answer about their own course, each asking you to write the policy in your own words. If you cannot put it in your own words, you have not read it yet, and week two is a much better time to discover that than week ten.

Where your data lives

In this browser, on this computer. It is not sent anywhere and there is no account. That also means nobody can recover it for you, so do one of these:

Link a file from the Setup tab if you are on Chrome or Edge. The journal is then written to a real file on your disk every time it changes. Put that file somewhere that syncs and it follows you between machines and survives a cleared browser.

Or download the JSON regularly and keep it safe. If you ever lose the browser copy, Import it and carry on.

Do not name students anywhere in this journal. “A student in my section” carries everything you need. A journal that names students becomes an education record, with rules about where it can be stored and who can read it, and you do not want that on your laptop or in a report you hand to someone.
Your data never leaves this computer on its own. It moves only when you export it, print it, or hand it to someone, and you do each of those deliberately.
