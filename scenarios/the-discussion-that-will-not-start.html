Giving feedback and leading discussions

The discussion that will not start

Read this before the session. You are not expected to have had this happen to you. You are expected to have thought about it for five minutes before anyone asks you what you would do.

You are a TA for CS 224, Data Structures and Abstraction, taught by Dr. Okonkwo. You run two discussion sections of fifty minutes each, both with the same material.

What happens

Your 8:00 section has fourteen people in a room with twenty-four seats. You planned three questions about when a linked list beats an array, and you are pleased with them.

You ask the first. Silence. You count to five in your head, then to ten, then it gets unbearable and you answer it yourself. The same thing happens with the second. By half past you are lecturing, which was not the plan, and two people have laptops open to something that is not the course. Near the end someone asks whether this will be on the exam. It is the only question all hour.

At 2:00 you run the identical plan with the identical worksheet. A student disagrees with your first question inside thirty seconds, someone else takes the other side, and you spend the rest of the session refereeing an argument you did not have to start. You end four minutes late because nobody wanted to stop.

Walking back you cannot work out what you did differently, because as far as you can tell you did nothing differently.

What you know

  • Both sections are capped at twenty-four. The 8:00 has fourteen regulars; the 2:00 is nearly full.
  • Attendance at section is not taken and participation is not graded.
  • Dr. Okonkwo told you at the start of term to "run it however works for you" and has not observed either section.
  • The worksheet was written by last year's TA. You have not changed it.
  • The first midterm is in two weeks.

What you do not know

  • Whether the 8:00 students have done the reading.
  • Whether the same fourteen people come every week.
  • Whether anyone has told them what a discussion section is for.

The three questions

  1. What would you do in the first five minutes?
    Not the ideal response. The one you would actually give, under time pressure.
  2. What does your course actually let you do?
    Everyone answers this one, about their own course. Your policy, or what your instructor has told you.
  3. Where did your group disagree?
    Do not smooth this over. People assisting different courses should not agree, and the disagreement is the most useful thing you can report.

What groups have said

Nothing published yet. Summaries of what groups decided are added at the end of each semester, with names and courses removed.

Every course, instructor and student in this scenario is invented. It does not describe a real person or a real incident.  All scenarios
