Reviewing your own teaching

The feedback you did not want

Read this before the session. You are not expected to have had this happen to you. You are expected to have thought about it for five minutes before anyone asks you what you would do.

Professor Lindqvist runs a mid-semester survey in CS 246, Computer Systems and forwards you the comments about your labs, with a note saying "thought you would want to see these, happy to talk any time".

What happens

There are eighteen responses. Twelve are positive or neutral and several are warm. You read them quickly.

Four say you go too fast.

Two say something harder. One is a single sentence: asking a question in this lab makes you feel stupid. The other quotes you. It says you told someone "we covered this last week", and you remember saying it, and you remember thinking you had said it kindly.

You read the two comments about eleven times. You are now unsure whether anything you thought you knew about how your labs go is true, and you have a lab to run on Thursday.

What you know

  • You run two labs, about sixty students in total. Eighteen responded.
  • The survey is anonymous and you cannot tell whether the two hardest comments came from the same person.
  • Six weeks of term remain.
  • Professor Lindqvist has not asked you to do anything about it and has not offered an opinion.
  • Nothing in your appointment or your evaluation depends on this survey.

What you do not know

  • What "too fast" refers to: the pace of the lab, the pace of your speech, or the assumed background.
  • Whether the person who wrote the second comment is still coming to lab.
  • Whether anything changed between the start of term and now.

The three questions

  1. What would you do in the first five minutes?
    Not the ideal response. The one you would actually give, under time pressure.
  2. What does your course actually let you do?
    Everyone answers this one, about their own course. Your policy, or what your instructor has told you.
  3. Where did your group disagree?
    Do not smooth this over. People assisting different courses should not agree, and the disagreement is the most useful thing you can report.

What groups have said

Nothing published yet. Summaries of what groups decided are added at the end of each semester, with names and courses removed.

Every course, instructor and student in this scenario is invented. It does not describe a real person or a real incident.  All scenarios
