Peer review of projects

The review that is not a review

Read this before the session. You are not expected to have had this happen to you. You are expected to have thought about it for five minutes before anyone asks you what you would do.

You moderate peer review of the final projects in CS 412, Introduction to Databases, taught by Professor Whitfield. Each student reviews three projects and every review is visible to the person who wrote the project.

What happens

You are checking reviews before they are released and three of them stop you.

The first reviewer has written, for all three of their assigned projects, a variation of Looks fine, good job, 10/10. Total length across all three reviews: forty-one words.

The second has written nine hundred words about one project, most of which argues that the author's whole approach is objectively wrong. It names the author six times. It is not abusive, and parts of it are technically correct, and reading it as the author would be miserable.

The third reviewer has flagged, politely and in a private comment to you rather than in the review, that a project appears to contain a substantial amount of code from a public repository with no attribution anywhere.

What you know

  • Peer review is worth ten per cent of the project grade, for the reviewer as well as the author.
  • Reviews are released to authors on Monday. Professor Whitfield wants final grades by Wednesday.
  • You have three days.
  • You have not read the course policy on using outside code in projects, and you are not certain there is one.
  • The third reviewer has asked you not to say who raised it.

What you do not know

  • Whether the reused code is permitted, and under what conditions.
  • Whether the author of that project knows it is a problem.
  • Whether Professor Whitfield wants to hear about any of this before Monday or after.

The three questions

  1. What would you do in the first five minutes?
    Not the ideal response. The one you would actually give, under time pressure.
  2. What does your course actually let you do?
    Everyone answers this one, about their own course. Your policy, or what your instructor has told you.
  3. Where did your group disagree?
    Do not smooth this over. People assisting different courses should not agree, and the disagreement is the most useful thing you can report.

What groups have said

Nothing published yet. Summaries of what groups decided are added at the end of each semester, with names and courses removed.

Every course, instructor and student in this scenario is invented. It does not describe a real person or a real incident.  All scenarios
