IN THREES · 15 MIN

Role play

Pick a course one of you actually assists. Take a role each: TA, student, observer.

Run a real office hours interaction for five minutes, then swap.

Observer report

  • What did you notice?
  • What would you recommend to your group?
The observer usually sees more than either participant felt. That is the whole argument for peer review later in the term.

The five you will meet

1. Wants the answer, not the learning

Focus on their thinking, not on supplying the result.

  • Ask them to explain the question in their own words
  • Ask what they tried and what they expected
  • The misunderstanding usually surfaces in their own explanation

2. Has not attended or read

Redirect to the material rather than re-delivering the lecture.

"That was covered Tuesday. Start there and come back with a specific question."

This is kind, not harsh. Re-teaching a lecture in office hours teaches everyone that attendance is optional.

3. Expects special treatment

Be explicit about the role rather than vague about the answer.

"Because I'm a TA I have to be fair to everyone, so I can't give you extra help outside office hours."

A complete sentence. It ends the negotiation without making it personal.

4. Blames you

The goal is to get onto the same side of the problem as the student.

Blame the situation rather than either person. "That question was ambiguous and it caught several people."

5. Emotional, insulting, aggressive

  • Do not passively accept abuse, and do not escalate
  • Calm first. "Let's work through it."
  • Move it out of the room if others are present
  • Tell your instructor afterward. Every time.

When you don't know

It is not about you knowing every answer. It is about the student's learning.

  • "I don't know. Let's find out." Then actually find out, in front of them.
  • Or: "Let me check and email you tonight." Then do it.
  • Never bluff. They can tell, and it costs more than the gap did.

Constructive and destructive

Destructive

  • Is about a person
  • Contains a judgment
  • Leaves nothing to do differently

Constructive

  • Is about an action you observed
  • Justifies the view
  • Points somewhere
"This loop runs one iteration too many" is usable. "Careless" is not.

Wise feedback

A framing statement that says two things at once: I hold a high standard, and I believe you can meet it.

"I'm giving you these comments because I have high expectations and I know you can reach them."

One sentence, and it changes how the rest of the comments are read. Worth putting at the top of a stack.

Where this shows up later

If you stay in academia

  • Reviewing papers, reading drafts, advising
  • More feedback than lectures, all career

If you go to industry

  • Code review is feedback with a diff attached
  • Surfacing the quiet objection is senior work
Ninety seconds to comment on work that is wrong in an interesting way. What you write decides whether they attempt the next one.

And if neither is yours: what do you want from the job you have?

The move

Ask before telling

It sounds passive. It is not.

Every one of those five situations improves when you ask a question first, because you find out what is actually wrong instead of solving the problem you assumed.
