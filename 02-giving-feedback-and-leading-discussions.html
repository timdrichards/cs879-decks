The three replies

"Hi, I'm stuck on question 3. I've been at it two hours and I don't know what to do. Can you just tell me if my approach is right?" Thirty lines of code pasted underneath.

A. "Line 12 should be i < n-1, not i <= n. Your base case is also wrong, it needs to return 0 when the list is empty. Fix both and it should pass."

B. "Great effort, you are close! Take another look at your loop bounds and think about edge cases. You have got this."

C. "Two hours is too long to be stuck, so I am glad you wrote. Before I look: what did you expect line 12 to do, and what did it do when you ran it? If you have not tried it with an empty list, try that first and tell me what happens. I am in office hours until four if it is easier out loud."

Score each out of 5, then write the one you would send.

What did you give them?

Hands up for each score. One reply at a time.

Reply A: it fixes the bug

A. "Line 12 should be i < n-1, not i <= n. Your base case is also wrong, it needs to return 0 when the list is empty. Fix both and it should pass."

  • It is not worthless. It is correct, it is fast, and the student is unblocked in one message.
  • What it costs: they learn the quickest route to an answer is you. Next time they send the code sooner and try less.
  • The tell: you wrote more of the solution than they did.
  • When it is right: they have shown their work and are stuck on something they could not have known. A library quirk, an ambiguous spec, a typo they cannot see. Then just answer.

Reply B: it fixes nothing

B. "Great effort, you are close! Take another look at your loop bounds and think about edge cases. You have got this."

  • It is not worthless either. The tone is right, and a student who feels dismissed stops asking.
  • What it costs: there is no information in it. They are still stuck, now at one in the morning, and now also feeling bad about it.
  • The tell: your reply would fit any submission in the course. If you could paste it under someone else's code, it says nothing about theirs.
  • The trap: this is the mistake new TAs actually make, because it feels kind and it is quick to write.

Reply C: it asks first

C. "Two hours is too long to be stuck, so I am glad you wrote. Before I look: what did you expect line 12 to do, and what did it do when you ran it? If you have not tried it with an empty list, try that first and tell me what happens. I am in office hours until four if it is easier out loud."

  • Names the cost. Two hours is too long, so the problem being solved is the being-stuck, not just the bug.
  • Asks for expected against actual. The most useful diagnostic question there is. Most of the time the bug surfaces in their own explanation before you say anything.
  • One concrete thing to try, not a checklist. They can do it in a minute and it produces a fact.
  • Offers a way out that is not more typing. Some problems die in ten seconds out loud and never in a thread.

Wise feedback

A high standard, plus a signal you think they can meet it. Either one on its own fails.
  • A was a standard with no signal.
  • B was a signal with no standard.
  • C was both, and it cost one extra round trip.

Why your section goes quiet

You ask a question. Nobody answers. Four seconds feels like a minute, so you answer it yourself, and the room learns that waiting works.

  • Wait longer than is comfortable. Count to ten. Someone breaks first, and it is not you.
  • Never answer your own question. Rephrase it or hand it to a pair, but do not fill the gap.
  • Have them talk to each other first. Thirty seconds in pairs, then ask a pair, not a person.
  • Ask something with more than one defensible answer. "What is the complexity?" gets silence. "Which of these two would you ship?" gets an argument.

Plan your next section

For the next section, lab or help session you run. Write all three down.

  • 1. The exact first question you will ask. Word for word.
  • 2. What you will do if nobody answers for ten seconds.
  • 3. One way you will get them talking to each other, not to you.

No section of your own? Write it for your next office hour with more than one student in the room.

In threes: would that make you talk?

  • 1. Read your first question to the other two. Just the question.
  • 2. They answer honestly: would you say something, or wait it out?
  • 3. If they would wait it out, fix the question together.
You will never have a more generous audience than this one. If it does not work here, it will not work at eight in the morning.

What does your syllabus promise?

In the syllabus of the course you are a TA for, find and write down:

  • 1. What it says about feedback, and how fast work comes back.
  • 2. What it says about your section or lab, if anything at all.
  • 3. One line you would change, and what you would change it to.

No syllabus? Write what you were told instead, and where you got it from. That is a finding, not a failure.

Find your pod

  • 1. Stand along the back wall, in order of the course number you TA. Lowest by the door.
  • 2. Count off one to five down the line. Your number is your pod.

Two courses? Stand at the one you spend more hours on. Not assigned to a course yet? Go to the far end.

Why we did it that way

None of that was arbitrary.

  • The line was a histogram. You saw the shape of this cohort before we broke it up.
  • Sorting put same-course people side by side, so counting off guaranteed you are not with them.
  • Every pod spans the curriculum. That is what makes a share-out worth sitting through.
  • One instruction did all of it. Nobody chose and nobody was picked last.
When you plan your own section, ask what the mechanic does for you. The best activities enforce themselves.
