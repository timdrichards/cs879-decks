Grading a data structures problem

In 100 to 150 words, explain when you would choose an ArrayList over a LinkedList, and when you would choose the reverse. Include a short code example showing one operation where the choice matters, and give the running time of that operation for each. (10 points)

The whole brief you get

  • "I just want to see that they get the tradeoff and can show it in code."
  • "The code doesn't have to compile, pseudocode is fine."
  • "Knock something off if the explanation is hand-wavy."
  • "Out of 10. It's early in the semester, so don't be too harsh."

The handout: Problem 1

Read the instructor's note aloud. It is two sentences and it sounds completely reasonable, which is the point. It is also roughly what any of them would write at 11pm. Do not editorialise yet. The next slide does that work.

What has it not told you?

  • How many of the ten points are the prose, and how many the code?
  • Does hand-wavy mean vague, or wrong?
  • Does the code have to be correct, if it does not have to compile?
  • The assignment says 100 to 150 words. The brief never mentions length. Does it count?
You answer all four today whether you decide to or not. Grading the stack answers them. The choice is whether you answer them once, on purpose, or forty times under time pressure.
Let the room find these rather than reading them out. Reveal the list after they have named two or three. Do not answer any of them. The room answers them in the next ten minutes and you write the answers on the board.

Build the rubric

Out loud, as a room. One yes-or-no line per point, ten points.

Not a rubric line

  • "Explains the tradeoff clearly"

A rubric line

  • "Names a case where the linked list wins and says why"

Type it into the Rubric Builder as we go, and you all leave with a copy.

Thirteen minutes. You type, they decide. Put it on the board or in the Rubric Builder on the projector. Push on anything that is not yes-or-no. Clearly is doing all the work in the left-hand card, and two graders will read it differently. Whatever they decide about length, write it down, or write down that there is no line. Both are decisions. Only one of them is a decision they will remember making.

Now grade three submissions

On your own first, then compare with the person next to you. Use the rubric we just wrote and nothing else.

  • A, then B, then C. In that order.
  • Write the number down before you talk to anyone.

The three submissions: Problem 1

Fifteen minutes, five a submission, keep it moving. The order matters. C is the double jeopardy case and it has to arrive while they are already grading. A is correct and 62 words against a stated 100 to 150. If the rubric has no length line, half the room deducts anyway. B reads like the strongest writer in the set and says LinkedList get(i) is O(1). A grader skimming for understanding gives it 8 or 9.

A had no length line to fail

The rubric never mentioned length, so there was nothing to deduct against. Is this an important criterion? Why or why not?

Practicum 1 asked you for about 300 words. Its rubric had no length criterion either. One of you wrote 204 words, met every line, and said so in the submission. They were right, and it is fixed as of today.
This is the slide where the course admits it made the same mistake. Do not skip it and do not soften it. Do not name the student unless they have told you it is fine. The useful question is not whether to deduct. It is whether the rubric said so before anyone read a submission.

One misunderstanding, three deductions

Submission C believes inserting at the front of an ArrayList is amortized constant time, having confused it with appending at the end. That one belief shows up three times:

  • In the explanation
  • In the conclusion about which structure to pick
  • In the code, which picks front insertion and labels it amortized O(1)
C lands around 4. B, which does not understand linked lists at all, lands around 8.
Put both numbers on the board next to each other and let the room sit with it. Then ask: is 4 the right grade for one misunderstanding? The principle is that the cost should be proportional to the size of the misunderstanding, not to how many places it surfaced. But it is a course policy question, so ask the instructor rather than inventing a rule. Different courses answer it differently and both answers are defensible.

Then C emails you

Read your own rubric as though you had never seen it, and answer one question: can I justify this number?

If you can

  • Name the rubric line
  • Name what their submission did
  • Say what earning the point looks like

If you cannot

  • Say so and change it
  • Then change it for everyone else with the same problem
A student who complains should not get a better outcome than a student who did not. That second step is the one new graders skip.
Put them in the student's seat before they answer. The reply that starts by defending the grader loses the room every time. If you can justify it, the mail is three sentences and it points at criteria that existed before you read their work. You are not arguing about whether they are clever. Say the second half twice. Quietly giving points back to the one who asked is the most common thing that goes wrong, and it is invisible until someone compares notes.

When no line covers it

Submission twelve does something the rubric is silent about. Do not improvise and keep going, because that is how the first eleven get graded against one standard and the last twenty-eight against another.

  • Stop. Write the new line, in the same yes-or-no form.
  • Ask whether it changes any score you have already given.
  • If it does, go back. Twelve is fifteen minutes. Thirty-five is an evening.
  • Keep the record of every call like this.
Short. This is the buffer block, so if the grading ran over, this slide alone is the version you keep. The record is what turns a decision into something you can defend, to a student or to the grader who inherits the stack.

Different course, same shape

In 100 to 150 words, explain why binary search requires its input to be sorted. Include a short code example showing a case where it returns a wrong answer on unsorted input, and give the running time of the search and of sorting first. (10 points)

In groups of two or three. Five minutes to write the rubric, before you read anything.

The handout: Problem 2 · Write it in the Rubric Builder

Groups of two or three, and keep them together for the swap on the next slide. Five minutes is tight on purpose. A group that argues about one line for five minutes has done the exercise. The brief here is shorter: the instructor wants the invariant rather than the rule, and the code has to demonstrate the failure rather than assert it.

Score, swap, score again

  • Score D, E and F against your group's rubric.
  • Swap rubrics with the group next to you.
  • Score the same three again, using theirs.
  • Did the two rubrics give the same totals?
Where the totals differ, find the line that caused it and read it aloud. It will be the one that was not yes-or-no.
Twenty minutes including the swap. Same groups of two or three. F produces the biggest spread, somewhere between 3 and 8. It is correct, well written, has the right running times, and explains why binary search is O(log n) rather than why sortedness is required. Excellent, and off target. Find the two groups that disagree most on F and have each read their own line out. That is the whole lesson in ninety seconds. The Rubric Builder makes a link that carries a rubric, so a group can send theirs rather than hand over a laptop.

So when do you revise a rubric?

  • Best: after a sample read, before you have committed a single score.
  • Second best: between batches, with a note about what changed.
  • Worst: halfway through, with no record.
Once grades are out, revising means regrading everyone, so the bar is higher. Still worth it when the defect is real. "You are right, that line was underspecified, here is the corrected version and I have applied it to the whole section" is a good sentence to be able to write.
Land the point that a regrade request exposing a genuine gap is not an attack. It is the cheapest rubric review they will ever get.
