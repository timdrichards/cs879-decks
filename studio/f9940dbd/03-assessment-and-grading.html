Pairs · 3 min

Our own rubric had a hole

Practicum 1 asked for about 300 words. The rubric had no line about length.

One of you wrote 204 words, met every line, and said so in the submission. You were right.

With the person next to you: write the missing line. It has to be yes or no.
Do not name the student unless they have said you can. Take two or three lines from the room and write them on the board. Pick one out loud and say it goes into the Practicum 1 rubric this week, then actually do it. Someone will propose "at least 300 words"; ask whether 290 fails. That argument is the unit in two minutes. A yes-or-no line per point is also the instruction for the rubric swap later, so you do not need to teach it separately.

A finished practicum, four ways

You mostly grade

  • Count regrade requests on one set. Write yes/no lines before the next set. Count again.

You hold office hours

  • Tally the visits that ended with you typing the code. Ask two questions first. Tally again.

You run a lab or discussion

  • Count who speaks. Add think time or pairs. Count again.

You make materials

  • Count setup questions in week one. Write the checklist. Count again next assignment.

One change, inside hours you already work. A number before and a number after. That is the whole project.

Every one of these comes from a focus somebody in the room picked for Practicum 1. Say that. The point to land: none of them needs you to lead a section. Nearly half the room does not, and the Friday prompts assumed they did. If someone asks what counts as "a number": a tally on a sticky note is fine. It only has to be the same tally before and after.
Threes · 9 min

Your focus in one sentence

Each person says their practicum focus in one sentence.

The other two ask one question: what would you count?

Mixed threes if you can: put someone who grades only with someone who runs a section. Walk the room with Boming. The people who come up blank here are the ones to catch before Friday, because the Baseline is due then.
Groups of four · 22 min

Rubric swap

  • Pick one real assignment from someone's course. No assignment? Use the week 1 let/const/var prompt.
  • Write four yes/no lines for it.
  • Swap rubrics with the group next to you. Grade two submissions using theirs.
  • Find one line that broke.
This is the one that gets the time. Let groups argue. Fallback material is the week 1 handout, unit01-grade-these-four.html on the deck site. They graded those four without a rubric in week 1 and their scores spread. Now they write the rubric that would have stopped it, so it is not a repeat. Report: one broken line per group, read aloud, and what broke it. Boming writes them on the board. Double jeopardy may come up on its own when a group finds one mistake costing points in two lines. If it does, that is the case from the unit page. The answer to give: it is a course policy question, so ask your instructor, and whatever the rule is, apply it to everyone.
