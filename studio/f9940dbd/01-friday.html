What does your course actually say?

You were asked to find the AI policy for the course you are TAing. Round the room, one word each.

Permissive  ·  Restrictive  ·  Silent  ·  could not find it

If it says nothing, that is a finding, not a failure to find.

This was reading they did on their own time, as a TA duty rather than 879 homework, so do not spend the session on it. One word each, moving fast. Count the silents out loud. In past terms it has been about a third of the room, and that number lands harder than anything on a slide. If someone did not look, have them say so rather than guess, and give them ninety seconds to check now while the others go. Do not make it a thing.

In threes: draft the reply

A student in your section emails: "Am I allowed to use ChatGPT for homework 3? I don't want to get in trouble." You answer within the hour.
  • Two minutes. Names, your course, one surprise.
  • Pick one. Whose course policy are you answering under?
  • Write one reply together. Real wording, not a summary.
Threes, not pairs. Three gives you someone who disagrees. The first two minutes are the introductions proper: names, the course they TA, one thing that surprised them. Start the timer and let it run, or it becomes five minutes. Then the part that changed. The group picks ONE member's course and answers under that course's AI policy, and writes ONE reply together. Do not let them write three separate emails: the disagreement about what the policy permits is the whole exercise, and they only have it if they are committed to the same policy. If a group's chosen policy turns out to be silent on the question, that is the most interesting case in the room. Tell them to answer anyway and to be ready to say what they had to invent. Say the line that is not on the slide: whatever the syllabus says, their reply becomes the policy that student experiences. Circulate. Ask each group which course they picked and why.

Read it out, and say why

Three groups read their reply aloud. Then: whose course was it, what does that policy say, and why did you answer the way you did?

Take the question you could not settle to your next staff meeting. If your course does not have one, ask your instructor however they have told you to reach them.
This is the session now, not a wrap-up, so it has twelve minutes. Three groups. For each one make them answer all three parts: whose course, what the policy says, and why they answered that way. The reasoning is the point. A group that reads a nice email and cannot say which line of the policy licensed it has not done the exercise. Ask the room whether anyone would have answered differently under their own course's policy. That contrast is the most useful thing they will hear today, because it shows them that 'the right answer' is course-specific rather than universal. The callout replaces what used to be an email to their supervising instructor. We dropped that: it puts twenty eight emails into instructors' inboxes in week one and a staff meeting is the natural place for it. Say it explicitly, because it is a real instruction and not a suggestion to forget on the way out. If you are behind, two groups instead of three.

Exit ticket

Whose AI policy did your group answer under, and which line of your reply were you least sure about?

Copy the prompt into question two. Closes at 10:00, five minutes after we finish.

Do not post this prompt anywhere. It is the only part of the ticket that cannot be answered from a dorm room, which is what makes the ticket an attendance record rather than a form. The prompt changed because the old one asked for the sentence they would send their supervising instructor, and that email is no longer part of the session. The second half is the half worth having. 'Which line were you least sure about' is where they admit the policy did not cover the case, and that is the data we want out of this term. Read it aloud once, start the timer, leave the slide up. Five minutes is plenty: they are describing something they did twenty minutes ago.
