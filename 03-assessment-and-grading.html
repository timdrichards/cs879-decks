Why do we grade?

Pick the one you actually believe. Not the one that sounds best.

  • To let students know how they are performing
  • To recognize effort
  • To get them to complete the work
  • To assign a percentage or a letter

Two ways to grade the same answer

Assessment by parts

  • Points for components. Fast, mechanical, easy to defend line by line.
  • Misses whether the response actually works as a whole.

Two ways to grade the same answer

Assessment by parts

  • Points for components. Fast, mechanical, easy to defend line by line.
  • Misses whether the response actually works as a whole.

Holistic assessment

  • Judges the response as one thing. Catches what a checklist cannot.
  • Harder to explain to the student who lost the point.

Neither is wrong. Switching between them halfway through a stack is.

Norm-referenced vs criterion-referenced

Norm-referenced

  • Judged against everyone else in the room
  • The same work earns a different grade depending on who else enrolled

Criterion-referenced

  • Judged against a standard fixed in advance
  • The same work earns the same grade every time
UMass expects criterion-referenced. The Undergraduate Education Council says instructors should assign both item grades and final letter grades against criterion-referenced standards.

"Grading on a curve"

Historically it meant norm-referenced grading.

Now it usually means adding points to lift the mean. Different thing.

Either way it is not a TA decision. Ask the instructor.

Think · Pair · Share · 6 min

Case one

The course you are TAing has no rubric for an assignment you are grading tomorrow. Forty submissions. Ten points each.

What do you do today?

Two minutes alone. Two with the person next to you. Then we take three answers.

What just happened

That was a Think-Pair-Share

Alone first, so everyone forms a position instead of deferring to whoever speaks fastest. Then pairs, so the risk of being wrong is one person and not thirty. Then the room.

What it costs: six minutes for one question. What it buys: participation from people who would never raise a hand cold, and you find out what they actually think rather than what the confident student thinks.

You can run this in a discussion section on Friday with zero preparation. It is the highest-value move in this course per unit of effort.

Build the rubric backward from the points

  • For each point available, write a yes-or-no item that decides whether it is earned.
  • If you cannot phrase it as yes-or-no, it will not survive contact with a real submission.
  • Read half a dozen submissions before scoring any. The mistakes you did not anticipate are the rubric lines you are missing.
  • Grade one problem at a time across all submissions, not one student at a time.

Case two: double jeopardy

A student misremembers a function name. It costs them points in four different questions.

Should it? What is the right total deduction, and how do you explain it to them?

Where that usually lands

  • Grade cost should be proportional to the size of the misunderstanding, not to how many places it surfaced.
  • But this is a course policy question, not yours. Ask your instructor what their rule is.
  • Different courses answer it differently and both answers are defensible.
The thing that is always yours: whatever rule you apply, apply it to everyone, and write it down the first time you make the call.
Think · Pair · Share · 5 min

Case three

You and another TA grade the same assignment. Your averages differ by eight percent.

A student's grade now depends on who opened their file. What now?

Calibrating a grading team

  • Write the rubric down before anyone starts
  • All grade the same small sample first, then compare and reconcile
  • Grade anonymously where the tooling allows it
  • Document every judgment call as you make it, so the next grader can follow it

This is also what prevents "but my friend wrote the same thing and got full marks," which is the complaint that escalates.

Feedback that does nothing

Don't

  • "Wrong."
  • "Did you even read the question?"
  • The same canned comment on every question in the stack

A red X already told them they were wrong. This adds nothing and costs goodwill.

Feedback worth the minute it takes

Do

  • Say what specifically went wrong, not that something did
  • Say what to do differently next time
  • Vary it. Different questions fail in different ways
Two parts to grading: correctness, which students usually work out themselves, and feedback, which they usually cannot. Only the second one needs you.

GenAI in your TA work

You may not

  • Use GenAI in TA duties without your course instructor's permission, even a UMass-approved FERPA-compliant tool
  • Enter any student data into a non-UMass tool. Names, schedules, work

You may, with permission

  • Refresh your own understanding of a concept
  • Draft an explanation containing nothing identifiable about a student
This course's own AI policy is more permissive than this. It does not apply to your TA work. Ask the instructor of the course you assist.

The move underneath all of it

Make your criteria explicit
before you apply them

When a student asks why they lost two points, you have three possible answers.

  • "That's what it felt like." Ends badly.
  • "You did worse than the others." Norm-referenced. Not what UMass asks for.
  • "The rubric gives the point when the loop terminates, and yours doesn't." Ends in a sentence.

Before next time

Take one assignment you are responsible for and write the rubric you are actually using. One yes-or-no line per point. Fifteen minutes.

Send it to your course instructor with one question: is this what you want?

You are not asking permission. You are asking them to correct you now, rather than after forty students have been graded against the wrong standard.

Two of you will find out your instructor has a rubric nobody sent you.
